India 2026

Best dates, peak times, viewing tips, and stunning locations — everything you need to catch shooting stars this year.

All dates fact-checked against IMO, NASA & AMS sources

There's a moment every meteor watcher knows: you're lying flat on your back in the cold dark, the Milky Way arching overhead like a river of ancient light, and then — a streak. A silent blaze across the sky, gone in less than a second, but burned into your memory forever. That's the magic of a meteor shower, and India in 2026 gives you some extraordinary chances to experience it.

This year's meteor calendar has a headline event that happens roughly once every fifteen years: the Perseid meteor shower peaking on the night of August 12–13 with a New Moon — meaning completely dark skies and potentially 100+ meteors per hour. But that's only one of multiple excellent windows. The Geminids in December also arrive under a thin crescent moon, offering 120–150 meteors per hour. If you're going to step outside and look up just twice in 2026, make it those two nights.

What exactly is a meteor shower? When Earth passes through trails of debris left behind by comets (and occasionally asteroids), those tiny particles — most no bigger than a grain of sand — slam into our atmosphere at tens of thousands of kilometres per hour. The friction causes them to heat up and burn brilliantly, creating the streaks we call shooting stars. The debris trails spread across space in predictable paths, so the same shower recurs every year as Earth crosses the same patch of sky.

India's geography is actually a hidden asset for stargazers. From the Thar Desert in Rajasthan to the high-altitude deserts of Ladakh and Spiti, from coastal Konkan to the forested hills of Coorg, the country hosts some of the planet's darkest skies. You don't need expensive equipment. You don't need a degree in astronomy. You need clear skies, an open field, a reclining chair or blanket, and about 30 minutes of patience. Let's get you prepared.

Detailed Guide to Every Meteor Shower

Quadrantids
January 3–4, 2026
Poor Conditions
Peak DateJan 3–4 (2:30 AM IST)
Expected Rate10–20 / hr from India
Moon100% Full Supermoon
Parent BodyAsteroid 2003 EH1
Viewing DirectionNortheast
DurationDec 28 – Jan 12

The Quadrantids open the year with theoretical potential — under a dark sky, this shower can deliver over 100 meteors per hour. But 2026's timing is cruel: a full Supermoon (the Wolf Moon) dominates the sky at exactly the same time, washing out all but the very brightest meteors. From India, the radiant in Boötes is also relatively low in the northern sky, limiting your view.

The silver lining: the peak window (21:00–00:00 UT on Jan 3/4, which is 2:30–5:30 AM IST on Jan 4) actually favours Asian observers. Patient watchers in northern India — particularly in Ladakh or Himachal Pradesh, facing northeast after 2 AM — may catch 10–15 bright, moon-resistant meteors per hour.

Best Places in India

Northern India locations (Ladakh, Manali, high-altitude Himachal Pradesh) where the northern horizon is wide open and winter skies are crystal clear. Avoid during January's cold snaps unless properly equipped.

Photography Tips

The full moon makes astrophotography nearly impossible. Try silhouette compositions — use the supermoon itself as the backdrop subject rather than fighting it. ISO 800–1600, f/2.8, 20-second exposures. Angle your lens away from the moon.

Beginner Verdict: Skip this one in 2026. The full Supermoon makes it a washout. Wait for the Lyrids in April.

Lyrids
April 22–23, 2026
Good Conditions
Peak DateApr 22–23 (1:30 AM IST)
Expected Rate15–20 / hr
Moon~25% Waxing Crescent
Parent BodyComet Thatcher (C/1861 G1)
Viewing DirectionEast / overhead
DurationApr 16 – Apr 25

The Lyrids are one of humanity's oldest documented meteor showers — Chinese astronomers recorded them over 2,500 years ago. Named after the constellation Lyra (home of the brilliant star Vega), these meteors are fast and occasionally produce dramatic fireballs. The good news in 2026: the waxing crescent moon sets before the radiant reaches useful altitude, giving you several hours of clean dark sky.

Maximum activity is predicted near 1:30 AM IST on April 23rd. This is one of the first truly rewarding showers after the long moon-plagued winter, and conditions in 2026 are among the best in recent years for this shower.

Interestingly, the Lyrids have surprised observers in the past with sudden outbursts reaching 100+ meteors per hour — while rare, it does happen. In normal years, expect a steady 15–20 per hour with occasional bright fireballs leaving persistent glowing trains.

Best Places in India

Coorg (Karnataka), Chikmagalur, Nilgiris, Kutch (Gujarat), and Spiti Valley are all excellent April choices. April nights in the Deccan highlands are pleasantly mild, making this a comfortable outing for families.

Photography Tips

ISO 1600–3200, 24mm or wider lens, f/2.0 or faster, 20–25 second exposures. Face east-northeast to frame Vega (the radiant) in the upper portion of your frame. April nights are warm enough for multiple-hour sessions.

Beginner Verdict: A great first meteor shower of 2026. Comfortable temperatures, decent rates, and friendly moon conditions. Plan a weekend camping trip around April 21–23.

May 5–6, 2026
Challenging
Peak DateMay 5–6 (3 AM – Dawn)
Expected Rate10–30 / hr from India
Moon84% Waxing Gibbous
Parent BodyHalley's Comet (1P/Halley)
Viewing DirectionEast, near horizon

The Eta Aquariids carry something remarkable: they are debris shed by Halley's Comet, the most famous comet in history. You're watching pieces of Halley burn up in our sky. Under a dark sky from India's tropical latitudes, the Eta Aquariids can deliver an impressive 20–30 meteors per hour — far better than northern European observers see, because the radiant in Aquarius rises higher above the horizon here.

Unfortunately, 2026's moon (84% illuminated, five days past full) makes this difficult. Your best strategy is to watch in the final dark hour before dawn — the period from about 4 AM to first light on May 6, when the radiant is highest in the eastern sky and the moon is low. Patient observers can still spot bright earthgrazers — those long, low-angle meteors that skim across the horizon dramatically.

Best Places in India

South India is ideal for Eta Aquariids — Munnar (Kerala), Coorg, Chikmagalur, and the Nilgiris all offer great southern horizons where Aquarius rises highest. Wake up at 3:30 AM and watch until dawn.

Beginner Verdict: Worth a pre-dawn attempt despite the bright moon, especially from south India. Set an alarm for 4 AM and see what Halley's Comet left behind.

The Delta Aquariids are actually decent performers from India's tropical skies — Aquarius rises high, and rates of 20–25 per hour are possible. But in 2026, the Full Buck Moon (98% illuminated) essentially nullifies the entire show. The vast majority of meteors, which are faint, will simply be invisible in the lunar glare.

The good news: the Delta Aquariids are active for over a month (July 12 – August 23), and overlap with the building Perseid activity. About two weeks before their peak — around July 14–20 — the moon will be much thinner, and you can catch some early Delta Aquariids alongside the first Perseid precursors. This overlap period can be rewarding.

Beginner Verdict: Skip the official peak. Instead, watch around July 14–20 under better moon conditions to catch a preview of the Perseid season.

Perseids ⭐
August 12–13, 2026 — BEST OF THE YEAR
Excellent — Must Watch
Peak DateAug 12–13 (1 AM – Dawn)
Expected Rate80–100+ / hr
Moon0% — New Moon!
Parent BodyComet 109P/Swift-Tuttle
Viewing DirectionNortheast (Perseus)
DurationJul 17 – Aug 24

Mark your calendar in red. Circle it. Set twelve alarms. The 2026 Perseid meteor shower is a once-in-a-generation alignment of circumstances: the peak falls on New Moon night — 0% illumination — meaning the sky will be as dark as it gets. This happens only about once in fifteen years for this shower. The last time conditions were comparable was 2018. After 2026, you'll wait until 2029 for anything similar.

The Perseids are debris from Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle, a comet with a 130-year orbit. They strike the atmosphere at 59 km/s — so fast that many leave persistent glowing trains visible for several seconds after the meteor itself vanishes. Under a truly dark sky on peak night, expect one or two meteors per minute, including frequent fireballs bright enough to cast shadows.

From India, the radiant in Perseus rises to a useful altitude by about midnight and climbs steadily through the pre-dawn hours. The sweet spot is 1 AM – 4 AM IST on August 13. The Milky Way will be simultaneously at its annual best, arching overhead in full glory — this is the night to drive to the darkest place you know.

💡 Monsoon Warning August falls deep within India's southwest monsoon season. Cloud cover is the primary threat in most of the country. Plan for multiple nights (August 11, 12, and 13 are all good) and have a backup location. The best bets: Ladakh and Spiti Valley (in the rain shadow, usually clear), Jaisalmer and Kutch (Rajasthan's monsoon is lighter in July but often clearing by mid-August), and Coorg/Chikmagalur (between rain bands at night). Check weather forecasts obsessively starting a week before.

Best Places in India

Hanle (Ladakh), Nako Village (Kinnaur), Spiti Valley, Jaisalmer, and Rann of Kutch are your best shots at clear August skies. In south India, watch weather closely — there can be breaks between rain bands. Even Munnar on a clear night will be spectacular under New Moon.

Photography Tips

This is the astrophotography event of 2026. Start with ISO 3200, f/2.0 or faster, 20-second exposures on a sturdy tripod. Frame the Milky Way in your composition and use the Perseus radiant in the upper third. Shoot continuous sequences and stack later for meteor composites. Use a remote shutter release. The absence of the moon means you can use even longer exposures without blowout.

Beginner Verdict: This is THE night of 2026. Plan months in advance. Book accommodations in dark-sky areas now. Even if you only watch one meteor shower in your life, make it this one this year.

The Draconids are unusual: they're best watched in the early evening rather than pre-dawn, since the radiant in Draco is highest soon after dark and actually moves lower as the night progresses. The moon conditions in 2026 are near-perfect (just 1% illuminated), but from India's tropical latitudes, Draco — being a far-northern constellation — barely clears the horizon, limiting visible rates to 3–5 per hour even on a good night.

That said, the Draconids have famously surprised observers with sudden storms (in 1933 and 1946, rates exceeded thousands per hour). While not predicted for 2026, the excellent moon conditions mean it's worth stepping outside for an hour on October 8 evening, scanning the northern horizon.

Beginner Verdict: Low-effort watch — just step outside after dinner on Oct 8 and look north for an hour. Don't rearrange your week for it, but enjoy the dark sky bonus before the Orionids begin.

The Orionids — another gift from Halley's Comet — are among the fastest meteors in the sky (66 km/s), known for their bright streaks and occasional fireballs. Orion rises magnificently above Indian skies in October, placing the radiant high and giving excellent geometric conditions. But an 80% illuminated moon in 2026 washes out most of the fainter meteors.

Still, the Orionids' speed means their brightest members punch through moonlight impressively. Watch from around 1 AM when the moon is lower and the radiant is high above Orion's shoulder (near Betelgeuse). Occasional exceptional years (like 2006–2009) brought 50–75 meteors per hour; recent years have been more modest.

Beginner Verdict: A modest moonlit show. Worth a casual look between 1–3 AM if you happen to be up, but not worth losing sleep over in 2026.

Leonids
November 16–17, 2026
Fair Conditions
Peak DateNov 16–17 (1 AM – Dawn)
Expected Rate10–15 / hr
Moon45% Half Moon
Parent BodyComet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle
Viewing DirectionEast (Leo)

The Leonids carry legendary status: in 1833 and 1966, the shower produced meteor storms of over 100,000 per hour — the sky reportedly looked like it was on fire. Modern Leonids are far more modest (10–15 per hour in regular years), but they remain the fastest meteors visible from Earth — entering at 71 km/s — producing brilliant streaks that sometimes leave persistent glowing trains for several minutes.

In 2026, a roughly half-moon (45%) will be present but sets before dawn, giving a few hours of cleaner sky in the pre-dawn hours when Leo rises high in the east. Watch from about 2 AM to 4:30 AM IST for the best activity. November nights in much of India are pleasantly cool, making this an accessible and enjoyable watch.

Best Places in India

Jaisalmer, Kutch (Rann), Coorg, and the Nilgiris are all good choices for November. Temperatures are comfortable, the monsoon is long gone, and skies are typically stable and clear.

Beginner Verdict: Worth watching in the pre-dawn hours. The moon interferes somewhat but not fatally. Great to combine with a November camping or trekking trip.

Geminids ⭐
December 13–14, 2026 — #2 OF THE YEAR
Excellent — Must Watch
Peak DateDec 13–14 (10 PM – 3 AM)
Expected Rate120–150 / hr
Moon21% Waxing Crescent
Parent BodyAsteroid 3200 Phaethon
Viewing DirectionEast / Overhead
DurationDec 4 – Dec 20

The Geminids are technically the most powerful annual meteor shower in terms of sheer numbers — up to 150 meteors per hour under perfect conditions. They surpass even the Perseids in peak rate. And unlike most showers, the Geminids come from an asteroid (3200 Phaethon) rather than a comet — a rare and scientifically fascinating fact. The meteors are often brightly coloured: yellows, oranges, and occasional greens streak across the sky at medium-slow speed, making them spectacular targets for photographers.

In 2026, a thin 21% waxing crescent moon sets by mid-evening, leaving the bulk of the night sky dark. The IMO predicts peak activity between Dec 13, 21:00 UT to Dec 14, 18:00 UT (which in IST is Dec 14, 2:30 AM to Dec 14, 11:30 PM). Watch from 10 PM onwards on the night of December 13, with activity building through midnight and peaking around 1–2 AM on December 14.

The Geminids are also one of the few showers that produce significant activity before midnight, since the radiant in Gemini rises early in the evening. This makes it accessible for families who can't stay up until 3 AM. Even from 10–11 PM, you may see 40–60 meteors per hour.

Best Places in India

Winter skies across India are typically crisp and exceptionally clear. Jaisalmer (Rajasthan), Rann of Kutch, Spiti Valley, Coorg, and the Nilgiris all offer stellar December skies. Temperatures in Ladakh and Spiti will be extremely cold (-20°C or lower at altitude) — go prepared or choose more accessible locations.

Photography Tips

ISO 1600–3200, f/2.0 or faster, 20–25 second exposures. The Geminids are slow enough to show colour on camera — long streaks of amber and yellow are common. Frame Castor and Pollux (the twin stars of Gemini) in your composition. Shoot sequences of 30-second exposures all night and stack for a stunning composite.

Beginner Verdict: Your second must-watch of 2026. Plan a December stargazing trip. The combination of warm chai, cold clear skies, and 2 meteors per minute is one of life's simple perfections.

The fastest meteors visible from Earth, producing brilliant streaks with a half-moon that fades before dawn. November post-monsoon skies across India are often superb. Worth a dedicated pre-dawn watch for 2–3 hours around 2–4:30 AM IST.

Despite the challenging moon phase, the Eta Aquariids rank fifth because India gets genuinely better viewing than most of the world for this shower — the radiant rises higher here than in Europe. A determined 4 AM wake-up on May 6 can still yield impressive earthgrazer meteors from Halley's Comet.

Section 04

The Complete Beginner's Meteor Watching Guide

You need surprisingly little to watch a meteor shower. Here's everything you actually need to know.

  • No telescope needed. This is perhaps the most important thing to understand. Telescopes have too narrow a field of view for meteors — you'll miss everything. Binoculars are also unnecessary. Your naked eyes, fully dark-adapted, are the ideal instrument.
  • Dark adaptation takes 20–30 minutes. When you first step outside, your eyes need time to adjust. Avoid all white light — phone screens, torches, car headlights. If you need light, use a red torch, which preserves night vision. Your pupils will dilate and your eyes will become up to 200x more sensitive to faint light after 30 minutes.
  • Find a dark, open site. The further from city lights the better. Even driving 30–40 km outside a major city makes a dramatic difference. Look for a site with an open, unobstructed view of as much sky as possible. Lie on your back and look straight up.
  • Bring a reclining chair or yoga mat. You'll be looking straight up for hours. A reclining camp chair, sleeping bag spread on the ground, or car bonnet are all popular options. Neck strain ruins the experience.
  • Dress warmer than you think. Even in summer, lying still outdoors at 2 AM gets cold — especially at altitude or in arid regions. Layer up. Bring a windproof outer layer, a warm hat, and a sleeping bag you can zip yourself into if needed.
  • Bring supplies. Thermos of hot chai or coffee, snacks, mosquito repellent (a must for August and May watches), and a power bank for your phone. Charge your camera batteries fully the day before.
  • Use a star app for orientation. Apps like Stellarium, Sky Map, or SkySafari help you identify where the radiant is. You don't need to stare at the radiant — meteors appear all over the sky — but knowing its location helps you face the optimal direction.
  • Watch for at least 90 minutes. Meteor watching requires patience. Peak rates can vary minute to minute. Give yourself at least an hour and a half, ideally starting before the predicted peak and extending through it.
  • Safety first. Tell someone where you're going. Go with a group if possible. Keep your phone charged. If you're in a wildlife-adjacent area (Coorg, Nilgiris, national park fringes), don't wander far from your vehicle. Carry a bright flashlight for emergencies.
The Milestone Moment Most first-time meteor watchers see their first shooting star within the first 10 minutes under a good dark sky. The instinct is to point and exclaim — then wonder if it was real. It was. And after your fifth or tenth, you'll start anticipating them, relaxing into the rhythm of the sky. That's when the magic really kicks in.
Section 05

Best Stargazing Destinations in India

Hanle, Ladakh

Home to the Indian Astronomical Observatory at 4,500m, Hanle is one of Asia's premier dark-sky sites. The high altitude means less atmospheric interference, and the Changthang plateau offers an unobstructed 360° horizon.

Best Season: June–September (road is open)
Dark Sky: Among India's darkest — Bortle 2
Access: Via Leh (7–8 hrs by road through Loma/Nyoma). Inner Line Permit required.
Note: Extremely cold nights even in August (5°C–10°C). Altitude sickness risk — acclimatise in Leh for 2+ days first.

Spiti Valley

The cold desert valley of Himachal Pradesh sits in the rain shadow of the Himalayas, giving it over 300 clear nights a year. The sky at Kaza, Langza, or Komic is breathtakingly clear, with the Milky Way visible to the naked eye as a textured, three-dimensional structure.

Best Season: June–October
Dark Sky: Bortle 2–3
Access: Manali–Kaza road or Shimla–Kaza road
Note: August in Spiti is perfect for Perseids — cold, crisp and clear while monsoon drenches the plains.

Coorg (Kodagu)

Karnataka's hill district offers forested ridgelines and coffee estates that provide remarkable dark-sky pockets in the Western Ghats. The terrain is more accessible than Ladakh, with comfortable accommodations and good infrastructure.

Best Season: Oct–Mar (post-monsoon)
Dark Sky: Bortle 4–5 from estates
Access: 4–5 hours from Bengaluru or Mysuru
Note: Excellent for Geminids (Dec) and Lyrids (Apr). Resorts like Evolve Back offer stargazing packages.

Jaisalmer, Rajasthan

The Thar Desert around Jaisalmer offers some of the flattest, most open skies in the country. Zero humidity, rare monsoon interference, and pre-dawn temperatures that are pleasant (except summer). The sand dunes of Sam provide a dramatic foreground for astrophotography.

Best Season: Oct–Feb (and Nov–Dec for Geminids/Leonids)
Dark Sky: Bortle 3–4 from dunes
Access: Flights and trains from major cities; well-touristed
Note: Many desert camps offer stargazing. December Geminids from Jaisalmer dunes = magical.

Chikmagalur

Another Western Ghats gem, Chikmagalur sits higher than Coorg with multiple peaks (Mullayanagiri, Bababudangiri) offering open sky access. The coffee country aesthetic combined with clear highland skies makes it a growing destination for astrotourism.

Best Season: Oct–Apr
Dark Sky: Bortle 4–5
Access: 5 hours from Bengaluru
Note: The Lyrids in April align perfectly with pleasant weather here.

Lens Recommendations

A wide-angle lens is your best friend. The Rokinon/Samyang 14mm f/2.8 is a popular affordable option. Any lens from 14–24mm at f/2.0 or faster will give excellent results. The Perseids favour wider angles since meteors can appear anywhere in the sky from a radiant high overhead.

Mobile Phone Photography

Modern phones (iPhone 15 Pro, Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra, Google Pixel 9) have genuinely capable night modes. Use Pro/Manual mode, set ISO to 3200–6400, shutter speed to 15–25 seconds, and mount the phone on a tripod or stable surface. Do not handheld — even a slight touch blurs everything. Some apps (NightCap, Manual Camera) give finer control. You likely won't capture individual faint meteors, but bright fireballs and the Milky Way are absolutely achievable.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The single biggest mistake is using auto-focus in the dark — your camera will hunt endlessly and miss everything. Pre-focus on a distant star in daylight and tape the focus ring. Second most common: forgetting to charge batteries. Cold temperatures drain batteries fast; carry spares in a warm pocket. Third: giving up after 20 minutes. Meteor watching requires settling in for the long haul.

The Composite Trick Professional meteor photos showing 30+ meteors in one image are composites — many exposures stacked on top of a single background sky. Take continuous 20-second exposures for 3–4 hours, then use free software like Sequator or DeepSkyStacker to align and combine frames. Your first attempt will probably result in something spectacular.
Section 07

Frequently Asked Questions

Can meteor showers be seen without a telescope?
Absolutely — and in fact, a telescope is the wrong tool for the job. Meteors streak across large portions of the sky in milliseconds. Only your naked eyes, covering a wide field of view, give you a real chance of catching them. A reclining chair under a dark sky is all the equipment you actually need.
What is the best meteor shower of 2026?
The Perseids on August 12–13 are definitively the best of 2026. A New Moon (0% illumination) coinciding with the peak creates a once-in-fifteen-years opportunity for completely dark skies. Up to 100+ meteors per hour are possible from a good dark site. The Geminids on December 13–14 are a close second with 120–150/hr under a thin crescent moon.
Can meteor showers be seen from cities?
The brightest meteors — fireballs — can occasionally be spotted even from city centres on peak nights. But for a proper experience, you need to get away from light pollution. Even driving 50 km outside a major city into rural darkness dramatically increases visible rates. Apps like Light Pollution Map help you find dark zones near you.
Are meteor showers predictable?
Peak dates are highly predictable — they recur annually within the same narrow window as Earth crosses the same debris stream. Exact rates are harder to predict, as they depend on how densely packed the debris stream is at the precise crossing point. Moon phase is perfectly predictable, which is why 2026's Perseid conditions were known years in advance.
Is meteor shower photography difficult?
It's more about patience than skill. With a DSLR or mirrorless camera, a tripod, and the settings described in Section 6, anyone can capture meteors on their first attempt. The main challenges are focusing in the dark (use manual focus), staying warm and awake for hours, and accepting that many long exposures won't contain a meteor at all. When one does streak across your frame, it's electric.
Does the monsoon affect meteor watching in India?
Yes — the Perseids (August) fall during the southwest monsoon, meaning cloud cover is the primary risk. The strategy: choose monsoon-shadow locations like Ladakh, Spiti, and parts of Rajasthan/Kutch where skies are typically clearer. Watch weather forecasts from 10 days out and stay flexible. Having multiple nights of coverage (Aug 11, 12, and 13) maximises your chances.
How long should I watch to see a good number of meteors?
Under a dark sky during a peak night, you should see your first meteor within 5–10 minutes. For a satisfying experience, plan for at least 90 minutes. Your eyes will be properly dark-adapted after 30 minutes, and meteor rates have natural lulls and bursts — patience pays off. Many experienced watchers spend 3–5 hours per session.
Which direction should I look?
Don't stare directly at the radiant constellation — meteors appear across the entire sky. The radiant is just the point they appear to originate from. Look roughly 90° away from the radiant (generally at the zenith — straight up) and take in as large an area of sky as possible. The only exception is if you want long-tailed earthgrazers; for those, look towards (but not directly at) the radiant when it's near the horizon.
Is there a total lunar eclipse visible from India in 2026?
Yes! On March 3, 2026, a total lunar eclipse with a deep magnitude of 1.155 is visible from India. Most of India will see the ending of totality near moonrise. Eastern India gets the best view. This is the Moon turning a deep red — the "blood moon" effect caused by Earth's atmosphere refracting sunlight onto the lunar surface. Clear your calendar for that evening.
What are the best apps for planning stargazing trips?
Stellarium (free, excellent star charts), Clear Outside (cloud and seeing forecast), Light Pollution Map (finding dark sites near you), SkySafari (comprehensive astronomy), and PhotoPills (planning astrophotography shots including Milky Way arch positions). For meteor specifics, the AMS (amsmeteors.org) and IMO (imo.net) websites publish the most authoritative data.
Section 08

The Sky Is Waiting

Of all the experiences India offers the curious traveller — the noise of Varanasi ghats, the silence of Rann of Kutch at sunrise, the cold clean air of a Himalayan pass — watching a meteor shower from a truly dark sky might be the most universally profound. It costs nothing beyond getting far enough from city lights to let the sky show itself.

In 2026, you have an exceptional calendar to work with. The Perseids in August under a New Moon are a genuine once-in-a-decade event. The Geminids in December offer one of the richest annual sky shows with almost no lunar interference. The Lyrids in April open the season beautifully for beginners. And threading between the meteor showers, a total lunar eclipse turns the moon blood red in March.

This is the year to plan that stargazing trip you've been putting off. To book a desert camp in Jaisalmer, drive up to Spiti with a tent, or simply find a dark hillside two hours from home. The universe, in all its indifferent magnificence, puts on this show every single year — and 2026 has arranged the lighting better than usual. Don't miss it.
